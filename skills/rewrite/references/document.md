# Document Grid

## Mission
Judge a whole document — not its sentences. Does the target reader find what they are looking for, at the moment they look for it, in the order they need it? Defects found here are cited `ISO 5.x.y`.

## Provenance and limit
Source: **ISO 24495-1:2023**, *Plain language — Part 1: Governing principles and guidelines*.

The standard is paywalled. Only clauses 1 to 4 and 5.1 to 5.2.3 were read, through the free official preview. **Clauses 5.2.4, 5.2.5, 5.3.2 to 5.3.8, 5.4.x and annexes A and B were not read.** Everything below is verified; nothing else may be attributed to the standard.

Direct consequence: **never present a rewrite as "ISO 24495-1 compliant".** Of the 22 guideline sub-clauses, only three concern the sentence and the word. The bulk of the standard's effort is **upstream** (who reads, why, in what context) and **downstream** (evaluating with real readers, the whole of clause 5.4). A rewriting skill covers 5.3.2-5.3.5 and can serve 5.2.2; it covers neither 5.1.x nor 5.4.x.

## The four principles (clause 4)
Quoted in English, the standard's language: no official translation into another language has been seen, and none must be invented.

- **Principle 1: Readers get what they need (relevant).**
- **Principle 2: Readers can easily find what they need (findable).**
- **Principle 3: Readers can easily understand what they find (understandable).**
- **Principle 4: Readers can easily use the information (usable).**

The 4th adjective is **usable**. "Actionable" circulates in secondary sources: it is an error.

The first three feed the fourth: a document is *usable* if its information is *relevant*, *findable* and *understandable*. The four are **interdependent**; the standard states that this is **not** a sequential process.

## Definition of plain language (3.1)
> Communication in which wording, structure and design are so clear that intended readers can easily **find what they need**, **understand what they find**, and **use that information**.

Three verbs, not four: relevance sits upstream, in the selection of content.

## Who the "reader" is (3.2)
Two notes that change the diagnosis:
- "Reader" includes **anyone who merely skims or scans** the document looking for a specific piece of information, anyone who hears it or touches it, and anyone it is read to — person or machine.
- A document may target **several audiences**; in case of conflict, **the need of the primary audience prevails**.

## ISO 5.2.2 — Ordering the information
Group related information and order it in a way that makes sense to the target reader, considering:

- **(a)** place the most important message where readers find it easily, **most often at the beginning**;
- **(b)** **introduce new information by building on what readers already know** — this is the given-new contract (`GN*` in `sentence.md`) raised to document level;
- **(c)** for instructions or a process, **chronological order**;
- **(d)** place the information **most** readers need before the information **only some** need;
- **(e)** if not following a process can cause harm, place **the warnings before** the instructions.

*Symptom (a).* The conclusion, the decision or the instruction arrives after three paragraphs of context.
*Symptom (b).* A section opens on a concept no earlier section has posited. Same diagnosis as GN2, at section scale.
*Symptom (d).* The special case is handled before the general case.
*Symptom (e).* A warning appears after the step it concerns, or in a footnote.

## ISO 5.2.1 — Findability and headings
The reader must be able to determine **quickly** what the document is about and whether it serves their purpose. Headings help them **predict what follows**, and are particularly useful in documents of **more than a few paragraphs**.

*Symptom.* Headings that predict nothing ("General", "Miscellaneous", "Notes"); a long document without headings; a first paragraph that does not say what the document is about.
*Fix.* A heading announces its content. A document of more than a few paragraphs carries headings.

## ISO 5.2.3 — Information design
Design techniques must show the relations between elements visually:
- **prominence** — important elements stand out more;
- **proximity** — elements that belong together are close;
- **similarity** — elements with the same function look alike.

*Symptom.* A warning at the same visual level as the rest; two related pieces of information separated by a section; two elements of the same nature formatted differently (one as a list, the other as prose).

## ISO 5.1.6 — Content selection, and its ethical clause
Put the reader's needs first; identify the questions they need answered; **leave out the content they do not need**; and select content ethically — accurate content, no false or misleading content, and **do not conceal or omit content that readers need to know**.

This is the normative version of Williams's ethical argument. **It forbids a rewrite from making an inconvenient piece of information disappear under cover of simplification.** A cut that removes a warning, a reservation, a condition or a responsible party is not a clarification: it is a change of content, and it is reported.

## What the standard says against readability formulas
Its introduction states that plain language emphasizes **the readers' success in using the document**, rather than mechanical measures such as readability formulas. This is an explicit position of the standard, not an interpretation.

It also distinguishes **plain language** (general public) from **easy language** (readers with comprehension difficulties): two different objects, not to be confused in a diagnosis.

## Declared scope of the standard (clause 1)
- Applies to writing for the general public, but also to **technical writing**, **legislative writing** and **controlled languages**.
- Applies to most written languages, **but gives examples only in English** — no normative example exists in any other language.
- Does not cover digital accessibility (referred to WCAG and EN 301 549).

## What this file does not cover
Not read, hence never to be cited as coming from the standard:
- **5.2.4** (headings that let readers predict what follows) and **5.2.5** (supplementary information kept separate);
- **5.3.2 to 5.3.8** — familiar words, clear sentences, concise sentences, paragraphs, images, respectful tone, cohesion. The lists circulating online ("active voice", "one idea per sentence", "avoid nominalizations") are plausible but **unverified**: for those points, use `sentence.md`, which cites its own sources;
- **5.4.1 to 5.4.4** — evaluation with real readers, i.e. the whole 4th principle;
- **annexes A and B**, including the checklist;
- any **official translation** of the principles into another language.
