# Sentence & Paragraph Grid

## Mission
Locate, in an existing text, the places that cost the reader dearly, and name the rule that explains it. Every rule carries an identifier (`W*`, `GN*`, `G*`): the diagnosis cites that identifier, never an impression.

## Provenance and status of the rules
- `W*` — Williams & Bizup, *Style: Lessons in Clarity and Grace*, 11th ed. (*Style: Toward Clarity and Grace*, Chicago 1990, is the short version of the same material: same principles, different lesson numbering and examples). These are **his** rules, restated.
- `GN*` — **derived** from Clark & Haviland, "Comprehension and the Given-New Contract" (1977). They are psycholinguists: they describe what the reader does, they write no writing advice. The derivation is owned here.
- `G*` — **derived** from Gibson, "The Dependency Locality Theory" (2000) / Gibson (1998). Same status. Gibson tests English, Japanese, Spanish, German, Dutch and Finnish; **not French** — the mechanism is structural, not lexical, and the extrapolation to French is owned here.

# Williams — subjects, verbs, positions

## W1 — Character as subject
**Symptom.** The first six or seven words of the sentence name no concrete actor: the subject is an abstraction, a nominalization, or the character is buried in a possessive or a noun complement — or absent.
**Fix.** Identify who does what, and move that actor up into subject position.
**Before/after (EN, Williams).**
- ✗ *Governmental intervention in fast-changing technologies has led to the distortion of market evolution and interference in new product development.*
- ✓ *When the government intervenes in fast-changing technologies, it distorts how markets evolve and interferes with companies' ability to develop new products.*

**Before/after (FR).**
- ✗ *La prise en compte des retours utilisateurs par l'équipe produit a conduit à une révision du périmètre.*
- ✓ *L'équipe produit a pris en compte les retours utilisateurs et a révisé le périmètre.*

**Hierarchy.** Williams is explicit: readers want actions in verbs, but they want characters as subjects **even more**. It is "the first principle of a clear style".

## W2 — Action as verb (nominalizations)
**Symptom.** An abstract noun derived from a verb (*-tion, -ment, -ance, -ity*) carries the real action, next to an empty verb (*be, have, do, make, carry out, conduct, lead to, allow*).
**Fix.** Five diagnostic patterns, transposable as they stand to French:

| Pattern | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Nominalization as subject of an empty verb | *The intention of the committee is to audit the records.* | *The committee intends to audit the records.* |
| 2. Nominalization after an empty verb | *The agency conducted an investigation into the matter.* | *The agency investigated the matter.* |
| 3. Two nominalizations linked by an empty verb | *Our loss in sales was a result of their expansion of outlets.* | *We lost sales because they expanded outlets.* |
| 4. Nominalization after *there is / il y a* | *There is no need for our further study of this problem.* | *We need not study this problem further.* |
| 5. Nominalizations chained by prepositions | *We did a review of the evolution of the brain.* | *We reviewed how the brain evolved.* |

In pattern 3, the empty verb hides a logical connector: plain cause (*because, since, when*), conditional cause (*if, provided that, as long as*), contradicted cause (*although, unless*). Putting it back is a gain in information, not only in style.

**FR, pattern 5.** ✗ *Nous avons procédé à une analyse de l'évolution de la consommation mémoire.* → ✓ *Nous avons analysé comment la consommation mémoire évolue.*

## W2b — Counter-rule: the nominalizations to keep
Four cases where denominalizing **degrades** the text:
1. **Short subject picking up the previous sentence**: *These arguments all depend on a single unproven claim.* / (FR) *Cette décision peut avoir des effets positifs.* — that is W3 at work.
2. **Short nominalization replacing "the fact that"**: *Her admission of guilt impressed me.* (rather than *The fact that she admitted guilt…*) / (FR) *Son aveu m'a impressionné.*
3. **Nominalization naming what would be the object of the verb**: *I accepted her request.* / (FR) *J'ai accepté sa demande.*
4. **Concept so familiar it is a virtual character**: *the migration*, *the election*, *taxation*, *the Equal Rights Amendment*.

## W3 — Old before new
**Symptom.** The sentence opens on information the reader cannot have yet, and closes on what they have just read.
**Fix.** Start with familiar information; end with what the reader cannot anticipate. Two sources of familiarity: the words of the previous sentences, and general knowledge of the subject.
**Before/after (EN, Williams).**
- ✗ *The weight given to industrial competitiveness as opposed to the value we attach to the liberal arts will determine our decision.*
- ✓ *Our decision will be determined by the weight we give to industrial competitiveness as opposed to the value we attach to the liberal arts.*

**Williams's explicit priority.** When W1, W2 and W3 conflict, **W3 wins**: for the reader, the cohesion of a passage outranks the clarity of its sentences taken one by one. A locally less tidy sentence that preserves the old→new junction beats a locally tidy sentence that breaks it.

## W4 — Cohesion ≠ coherence
A distinction systematically flattened in second-hand citations. It commands two different diagnoses:

- **Cohesion** — you judge a **pair** of sentences: how one ends, how the next begins. Two puzzle pieces that fit together. Belongs to W3.
- **Coherence** — you judge a **whole passage**: how all its sentences begin, cumulatively. The picture on the puzzle box.

**A text can be perfectly cohesive and totally incoherent.** Three causes of incoherence: the sentence subjects are unrelated to one another; the sentences share neither a theme nor an idea; no sentence says what the whole is about.

**Test.** List the grammatical subjects of every sentence in a paragraph. If they do not form a small set of related ideas, the paragraph is incoherent — even if every junction is smooth.
**Fix.** Short, concrete, familiar topics, **repeated**. Williams is blunt: do not vary your subjects for the sake of variety.

## W4b — Simulated coherence
**Symptom.** *thus, therefore, moreover, however* (FR: *ainsi, donc, par ailleurs, toutefois*) sprinkled with no real logical link behind them.
**Fix.** A *but* when you contradict or qualify, a *therefore* to close a line of reasoning, and no more than a few occurrences per page. If the sentences are cohesive and the passage coherent, the reader does not need them. Adding a connector never repairs an incoherence: it masks it.

## W5 — Stress position (end of sentence)
**Symptom.** The last three or four words of the sentence do not deserve the emphasis.
**Fix.** Cut the ending if it is redundant; move to the end what you want to stress; reorganize with *what… is*, *there is*.
**Before/after.** ✗ *Sociobiologists claim that our genes control our social behavior in the way we act in situations we are in every day.* → ✓ *…control our social behavior.*
**General principle.** Go **from simplicity to complexity**: a short introduction, a short concrete subject, a specific action verb — after which the sentence may run for several lines.

## W6 — Passive
**Symptom.** A passive where the agent matters, or where the passive breaks the flow. **Not** "there is a passive".
**Fix — Williams's three questions:**
1. Must the reader know who is responsible for the action? If not, the passive is the right choice. *Sensitive documents must be kept in a safe.*
2. Does the active or the passive help move more smoothly from one sentence to the next? (→ W3)
3. Does the active or the passive give a more consistent point of view? Pick a point of view and hold it; the defect is not the passive, it is the unmotivated alternation.

**The passive's reason to exist.** Williams writes that the biggest reason for its existence is to let us order sentences so that they chain. Canonical example:
- ✗ active: *…scientists studying black holes in space. The collapse of a dead star into a point perhaps no larger than a marble creates a black hole.*
- ✓ passive: *…scientists studying black holes in space. A black hole is created by the collapse of a dead star into a point perhaps no larger than a marble. So much matter compressed into so little volume changes…*

Here the passive does **two** things: old at the head, new at the end where the next sentence picks it up.

**Active is mandatory** for metadiscourse verbs: ✗ *The terms of the analysis must be defined.* → ✓ *We must define the terms of the analysis.*

## W7 — Get to the subject quickly, then to the verb and the object
**Symptom.** More than ~7-8 words before the main verb; a long introductory subordinate clause; a long subject containing a nominalization or a relative clause.
**Fix.** If the introduction is long, move it to the end; if it does not fit there, make it a sentence of its own. If the subject exceeds 7-8 words and contains a nominalization, denominalize and find it a subject.
**Before/after.**
- ✗ *Abco Inc.'s understanding of the drivers of its profitability in the Asian market for small electronics helped it pursue opportunities in Africa.*
- ✓ *Abco Inc. was able to pursue opportunities in Africa because it understood what drove profitability in the Asian market for small electronics.*

**Named exception.** The periodic style deliberately stacks subordinate clauses to delay and amplify the main clause (*When a society spends more on its pets than on its homeless, … it has lost its moral center*). Intended, so not to be "fixed".

## W8 — Subject-verb and verb-object interruption
**Symptom.** An interpolation between the subject and its verb, or between the verb and its object.
**Fix.** Move the interpolation to the head or the end, depending on whether it attaches to what precedes or to what follows. Converges with G3.
**Before/after.**
- ✗ *Some scientists, because they write in a style that is impersonal and objective, do not easily communicate with laypeople.*
- ✓ *Since some scientists write in a style that is impersonal and objective, they do not easily communicate with laypeople.*
- (FR) ✗ *Nous devons développer, si nous voulons rester compétitifs face aux autres acteurs de la région, un socle de compétences…*
- (FR) ✓ *Si nous voulons rester compétitifs face aux autres acteurs de la région, nous devons développer un socle de compétences…*

**Short interruptions are accepted**: *Some researchers deliberately write in an impersonal style* poses no problem.

## W9 — Hedges and intensifiers
These are not faults: they are ethos settings. Touching them changes the meaning, not the form.

- **Hedges**: *usually, often, sometimes, almost, virtually, possibly, perhaps, apparently, somewhat, to a certain extent*; *most, many, some*; *may, might, can, could, seem, tend to, appear, suggest, indicate*. (FR: *souvent, parfois, presque, quasiment, possiblement, sans doute, apparemment, plutôt* ; *la plupart, certains* ; *peut, pourrait, semble, tend à, suggère, indique*.)
- **Intensifiers**: *very, quite, clearly, obviously, undoubtedly, certainly, of course, indeed, inevitably, always, literally*; *key, central, crucial, fundamental, major, essential*; *show, prove, establish*, and *as everyone knows*, *it is clear that*. (FR: *très, tout à fait, clairement, évidemment, indubitablement, bien sûr, en effet, inévitablement, toujours, littéralement* ; *clé, central, crucial, fondamental, majeur, essentiel* ; *montre, prouve, établit*, *comme chacun sait*, *il est clair que*.)

**The counter-intuitive point, and the most important one here: the most common intensifier is the absence of a hedge.** Removing the hedges does not make a text neutral, it makes it aggressive. Williams demonstrates it by "de-hedging" Watson & Crick's 1953 paper (*We wish to suggest a structure…*): the text becomes more concise **and arrogant**.

**Symptoms.** (a) stacked hedges (*it would seem that there might be some elements tending to suggest that…*); (b) a bare assertion where the evidence is partial; (c) intensifiers at the head of a clause.
**Fix.** Aim for the middle register: *these data suggest that…* rather than *these data prove that…* or *it would seem that there are a few elements suggesting that…*. Intensifiers go first: a reader who reads *it is clear that*, *there is no doubt that* reflexively thinks the opposite.

## W10 — Concision
Six operations, in this order: delete words that mean nothing; delete words that repeat the meaning of other words; delete what is implied; replace a phrase with a word; turn negatives into affirmatives; delete useless adjectives and adverbs.

**W10 is not a length target**: it is the removal of emptiness. It never justifies removing a piece of information, a hedge (W9) or a connector (W2, pattern 3).

# What Williams says AGAINST applying his rules mechanically

This section is not an appendix: it is what prevents over-correction. A rule applied without it produces a flat and false text.

1. **Principles, not prescriptions.** They serve to **predict** how a reader will judge the text, then to decide whether to revise. Williams adds that they belong to **revision, not drafting**: "if you think about these principles as you draft, you may never finish drafting".
2. **Do not impose them sentence by sentence.** They serve to **spot** the sentences likely to trouble the reader, not to go over every one.
3. **The passive is often the best choice** (W6).
4. **Some nominalizations must stay** (W2b, four cases).
5. **"Clarity, Not Simplemindedness".** Against the rule "every sentence must be under 15-20 words": many mature ideas do not fit that format. Williams devotes two whole lessons to **lengthening** sentences that are too short and too simple.
6. **Think about sentence length only if they all run over ~30 words, or all under 15.** In between, variation comes by itself.
7. **Cohesion outranks sentence-level clarity** (W3).
8. **There is irreducible complexity.** The rule is not "always simplify" but: make ideas *no simpler than they deserve, but no more difficult than they have to be*.
9. **Indirection out of kindness is not opacity.** *Our funding was not granted after all* for *you did not get the job* is motivated by consideration. "Disambiguating" that is a fault.
10. **The choice of subjects is an ethical choice.** Williams analyzes an automobile recall letter whose subjects are the vehicle's parts, never the driver, and whose worrying actions are nominalized or passivized. Direct consequence: **W1, W2 and W6 change who is designated as responsible**, not only readability. That shift is reported, it is not made in silence.

# Clark & Haviland — the given-new contract

## The contract
Speaker and listener have an implicit agreement on how known information and new information appear in sentences. Its core is the **antecedence maxim**:

> "Try to construct your utterance such that the listener has one and only one direct antecedent for any given information and that it is the intended antecedent."

Three conditions: **one** antecedent, **only one**, and **the intended one**.

The reader applies a three-step procedure: (1) isolate in the sentence what is given and what is new; (2) search memory for a direct antecedent; (3) integrate the new by attaching it to that antecedent.

## When the antecedent is missing: three outcomes, three treatments
A point systematically lost in second-hand citations — the three do not have the same status.

1. **Bridging.** The reader builds an **indirect** antecedent by inference. They assume the author intended it. Costly but **legitimate**, and sometimes intended. `→ do not fix systematically.`
2. **Addition.** No bridge can be built: the reader creates a fresh node. This is what happens at the opening of a text or a section (*The old woman died.*). **Normal.** `→ never "repair".`
3. **Restructuring.** The author split given and new badly; the reader has to re-split it themselves to save the sentence. Example: *Agnes saw somebody. It was Agnes who saw Maxine.* This is the only case of a **breach** of the contract, and the only one where the reader blames the author. `→ always fix.`

## The cost, measured — and why no metric sees it
Self-paced reading, time on the target sentence:

| Condition | Time |
|---|---|
| Direct antecedent — *Horace got some **beer** out of the car. **The beer** was warm.* | 835 ms |
| Indirect antecedent — *Horace got some **picnic supplies** out of the car. **The beer** was warm.* | 1016 ms |

**≈ 180 ms of extra cost.** A replication controlling for mere word repetition (*Horace was especially fond of beer. The beer was warm.* — the word is there, no individuated quantity is posited): 1031 vs 1168 ms, **≈ 140 ms**.

Two decisive consequences:
- **Both sequences are perfectly acceptable.** The extra cost is not a detectable fault — just a slower text. No readability formula, no grammar checker sees it. That is precisely what this grid exists to see.
- **The trigger is not the word, it is the individuation of the referent.** A lexical repetition therefore does not guarantee cohesion.

## Derived rules

**GN1 — One antecedent, only one, and the right one.**
*Symptom.* A definite (*the*, *this/that*, *it*, *they*; FR: *le/la/les*, *ce/cette*, *il/elle*, *cela*) whose antecedent (a) does not exist before, (b) exists in two competing copies, or (c) exists but is not the intended one.
*Fix.* (a) introduce the referent, or switch to an indefinite; (b) disambiguate **by repeating the exact term**, never with a synonym; (c) bring the intended antecedent closer or rename it.
*Before/after (FR).*
- ✗ *L'équipe a revu l'architecture du service. **Le cache** doit être invalidé à chaque déploiement.* → forced bridge: "the architecture includes a cache".
- ✓ *L'équipe a revu l'architecture du service, qui repose sur **un cache applicatif**. **Ce cache** doit être invalidé à chaque déploiement.*

**GN2 — Every sentence of the paragraph opens on an argument already posited.**
*Symptom.* Sweep the paragraph sentence by sentence: does the head noun phrase have a direct antecedent in the two previous sentences, or in the target reader's general knowledge? One isolated failure = a bridge, acceptable. **Three failures in a row = paragraph to restructure.**
*Fix.* Reorder the sentences, or spell out the missing link — often it is enough to move a phrase from the end of one sentence to the start of the next, adding nothing.

**GN3 — One concept, one word.**
*Symptom.* Elegant variation: *the service* / *the component* / *the module* / *the building block* for the same thing.
*Fix.* Fix one term and repeat it. The antecedence maxim demands a match on the given information; a synonym forces a gratuitous bridge.
**This rule flatly contradicts the stylistic instinct — especially strong in French prose — of avoiding repetition.** It applies anyway: repeating a technical term is cohesion, not poverty.

**GN4 — Do not "repair" an opening.**
*Symptom.* First sentence of a text or a section without an antecedent.
*Fix.* None. This is the "addition" case: the reader creates the node. Over-explaining adds weight without gain.

## GN vs W3 — the difference, plainly
"Old before new" (W3) is a **placement heuristic** that maximizes the probability that the antecedent search succeeds. It is not the contract. Consequences:
- You can obey W3 **and still breach** the maxim, if the element placed at the head has no antecedent, or has two.
- You can break W3 **without breaching** the contract, when the antecedent comes from the reader's general knowledge.

| | Clark & Haviland | Williams |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | What the reader does | What the writer must do |
| Object | The **antecedent** in memory (text **or** world knowledge) | The **position** of the information in the sentence |
| Scope | The whole discourse | The junction of two sentences (cohesion), the chain of subjects (coherence) |
| Sanction | A bridge, an addition, or a breach | A sentence "that does not flow" |

# Gibson — dependency locality theory

## The cost
Connecting a word to the structure already built costs as a function of the **distance** between the dependent and its head. The unit of distance is **not** the number of words: it is the **number of new discourse referents** (entities and events) introduced in the intervening zone.

To which a **storage** cost is added: one unit per syntactic head still expected for the sentence to be grammatical.

## Why center embedding costs disproportionately
- (1a) *The reporter disliked the editor.*
- (1b) *The reporter [who the senator attacked] disliked the editor.* — heavy but readable
- (1c) # *The reporter [who the senator [who John met] attacked] disliked the editor.* — **unworkable for most readers**

In (1c), the single word *attacked* concentrates the cost: integration with its subject, plus co-indexation with the relative pronoun four new referents away. The difficulty does not come from the length of the sentence, it comes from the **concentration of the cost on one word**.

## The decisive control
- (2) *John met the senator [who attacked the reporter [who disliked the editor]].*

**Same words, same ideas, same relations, comparable length — no difficulty.** The relatives are on the right, not embedded.

This is the proof to cite against any temptation to cut: **the cost collapses through a mere change of order, with nothing removed.** No readability formula distinguishes (1c) from (2).

## Subject relatives vs object relatives
- subject: *The reporter **who sent** the photographer to the editor hoped for a good story.*
- object: *The reporter **who the photographer sent** to the editor hoped for a good story.*

Object extraction is harder on converging measures: reading time, online lexical decision, accuracy on probe questions, cerebral blood flow in the language areas; and aphasic patients answer reliably on subject relatives, not on object relatives.

## Derived rules

**G1 — Bring the subject closer to its verb.**
*Symptom.* Count the referents (entities + events) between the subject and the main verb. ≥ 3 → revise.
*EN.* ✗ *The reporter who the senator who John met attacked disliked the editor.* → ✓ *John met the senator who attacked the reporter who disliked the editor.*
*FR.* ✗ *Le rapport que l'auditeur que la direction avait mandaté a remis critique la procédure.* → ✓ *La direction a mandaté un auditeur, qui a remis un rapport critiquant la procédure.*

**G2 — Branch to the right rather than in the center.**
A relative clause embedded in the subject is a center embedding; the same relative on the complement is a right branch, almost free.
*FR.* ✗ *Les fichiers que le script que nous avons livré hier génère sont invalides.* → ✓ *Le script livré hier génère des fichiers invalides.*

**G3 — Take the interpolations out.**
Any parenthesis, apposition or precision placed between two dependent elements adds exactly its count of referents to the cost. Move it to the head or the end. Converges with W8.
*FR.* ✗ *La migration, qui a été décidée en comité de pilotage après trois mois d'analyse comparative des offres, échoue sur les jeux de données volumineux.* → ✓ *La migration échoue sur les jeux de données volumineux. Elle avait été décidée en comité de pilotage, après trois mois d'analyse comparative des offres.*

**G4 — Short before long.**
Every complement placed before another pushes the latter's head further away. The light complement first, the heavy one last. Converges with W5 and with "new at the end" (W3): the same constraint, three angles.
*FR.* ✗ *Le service transmet à l'ensemble des partenaires référencés dans le registre national les identifiants.* → ✓ *Le service transmet les identifiants à l'ensemble des partenaires référencés dans le registre national.*
*EN.* ✗ *We sent to every customer who had registered before the cutoff date a confirmation email.* → ✓ *We sent a confirmation email to every customer who had registered before the cutoff date.*

**G5 — Prefer the subject relative to the object relative.**
*FR.* ✗ *Le ticket que l'astreinte a escaladé…* → ✓ *L'astreinte a escaladé le ticket ; celui-ci…*

**G6 — Do not confuse G1-G5 with "cut the sentences".**
The DLT says nothing against long sentences. It says something against **long dependencies**. Gibson's version (2) is as long as (1c) and costs a fraction. Cutting for the sake of cutting improves nothing, and often breaks the old→new chain (GN1, GN2).

# The readability-formula trap

The section that matters most, because it is the mistake this skill exists not to make.

## What the formulas compute
| Formula | Inputs — **nothing else** |
|---|---|
| Flesch Reading Ease, Flesch-Kincaid | words per sentence, syllables per word |
| Gunning Fog | words per sentence, % of words with ≥ 3 syllables |
| SMOG | number of polysyllables, number of sentences |
| Coleman-Liau, ARI | characters per word, words per sentence |

Three families of variables: word length, sentence length, crossing a syllable threshold. **No formula has a variable representing word order, a connector, an antecedent, a cohesion chain, a syntactic structure, a paragraph, a heading, a list or a table.**

Three verifiable consequences:
- **Shuffling the words of a sentence does not change its score** (Stockmeyer 2009). On Flesch and Flesch-Kincaid, Joyce's *Ulysses* comes out slightly "easier" than Beatrix Potter's *The Tale of Peter Rabbit*.
- **Two sentences with the same score, not the same difficulty** (Redish 2000): *I wave my hand.* / *I waive my rights.*
- **Bulleted lists are penalized.** The formulas count sentences between two periods: a list without final punctuation is read as one endless sentence, while usability tests show the opposite.

## The mechanism of the trap, in four steps
1. **The score is an average of two lengths.** Cutting a sentence in two lowers `words/sentences` mechanically, without touching the content — "it is enough to add periods, no writing skill is required" (Schriver 2000).
2. **The cut destroys the connector that carried the relation.** Davison et al. (1980), on a corpus of real adaptations: when coordinated clauses are split, **all** the conjunctions disappear. "All that remains is the weaker degree of information that one sentence precedes or follows another."
3. **The reader then has to produce a bridging inference** — the relation becomes "inferable but not directly stated", and reconstructible "but with less certainty". This is exactly the `bridging` of Clark & Haviland described above.
4. **The formula records the improvement, the reader absorbs the degradation.**

**The canonical example** (Bruce, Rubin & Starr 1981, after Davison et al.):
- Original: *If given a chance before another fire comes, the tree will heal its own wounds **by growing new bark over the burned part**.*
- "Simplified" adaptation: *If given a chance before another fire comes, the tree will heal its own wounds. **It will grow new bark over the burned part.***
- The authors' verdict: the modified text has shorter sentences, hence a better score — but the reader now has to infer that the new bark **is the mechanism** of the healing, which the original stated. "Thus, the adapted text may actually be more difficult than the original."

## The experimental evidence
- **Charrow & Charrow (1979)**, jury instructions rewritten by targeting linguistic constructions (nominalizations, passives, negations, organization): comprehension from 45% to 59%. Reported by Redish (2000): "In many cases, their revisions got better comprehension scores but **worse** readability scores. (This happened primarily because they **added words** to show the relationships among the information items.)"
- **Duffy & Kabance (1982)**, the negative control: four versions of a text, a drop of **six grade levels** in the score, 1,169 subjects, a protocol designed to maximize the expected effect. **Three experiments out of four show no significant improvement.** The explanation (Olsen & Johnson 1989): by breaking the long sentences, the rewrites introduced new grammatical subjects and destroyed cohesion — the intended and unintended effects cancelled out.
- **Britton & Gülgöz (1991)**: a ~1,000-word text rewritten by mechanically repairing the points where it forces an inference (argument overlap restored, known information before new information, referents made explicit). Higher free recall, higher learning per unit of time, mental representation closer to the author's intent. This is the demonstration of the reverse mechanism: **repairing cohesion lengthens the text**, hence degrades the score.
- **Klare (1976)**, cited by Redish: of 36 studies trying to improve comprehension by improving the score, **only half succeed**, and the score had to be moved by **6.5 grade levels** on average.

## What the formulas' own authors say
Klare, Flesch and Gunning all wrote that their formulas **must not be used to revise**: they are correlations, they indicate no cause. Klare (1979): optimizing a text for its readability score is **holding a match under the thermometer to warm the room**. And: "the temptation simply to substitute an easy word for a hard one, to cut sentences in half, or to eliminate prepositional phrases even when they are needed **may produce exactly the opposite of the desired result**."

ISO 24495-1:2023 takes the same position in its introduction: plain language is about **the readers' success in using the document**, rather than about mechanical measures such as readability formulas.

## What the formulas legitimately do — and the usable rule
This is not an indictment. Surface variables remain, on corpora, the best **simple** predictors of difficulty as measured by comprehension tests, and a rough ranking by level stays above chance.

The defensible use is **asymmetric**, and Redish states it this way: a very bad score probably signals a real problem — it is a red flag that licenses opening the text. **A good score licenses nothing.**

> **A readability formula is a sensor, never an objective function.** Any rewrite undertaken *to move the score* optimizes the index at the expense of what it indexes.

Concretely, for this skill: never measure, never aim at, never cite a score as the justification of a rewrite. The justification is always a rule of this grid — `W*`, `GN*`, `G*` — and the rewritten text has every right to be longer than the original.

# Order of application

When several rules apply to the same passage and contradict one another:

1. **GN1 / GN2 first** — a missing or ambiguous antecedent costs more than a badly chosen subject, and fixing it often moves the rest.
2. **W3 next** — cohesion outranks sentence-level clarity, Williams says so himself.
3. **G1-G5 then W1, W2, W5-W8** — the local rewrites.
4. **W10 last**, and only on what carries nothing.
5. **W9 never without reporting it** — touching a hedge or an intensifier changes the meaning.
